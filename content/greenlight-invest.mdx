<Section no="01" label="Starting line" title="Lots of words, not much to go on.">
  <Lead>
    When I joined, Invest looked like a balance sheet somebody remade in

  </Lead>

  The one place you could get help was a toggle called learn mode. Turn it
  on and a purple lightbulb went off and added detail: what a Morningstar
  rating is, what an expense ratio means. It was the right instinct. In
  practice it was buggy, it only covered part of the page, and once it was
  on the whole thing got even longer and felt like homework.

  <BeforeSheet />
</Section>

<Section no="02" label="The first lap" title="A bit smarter, but not simpler.">
  <Lead>
    We showed kids our new stock details screen: cleaner charts,
    better-formatted financials, and content we tried to tailor to their
    grade level. They still didn&rsquo;t know what the company was or what
    to do with any of it.
  </Lead>

  Our first round of research, with the children of Greenlight employees,
  kind of reset the project. The old Invest gave you company facts with no
  path from *I wear Nike* to *I want to own a piece of it.* It was too much,
  and nothing on the page connected to what they already knew.

  <LapFlow />
</Section>

<Section no="03" label="Speaking the same language" title="Making it make sense.">
  <Lead>
    Kids needed someone to tell them what a company does and how it makes
    money, in words they&rsquo;d actually use.
  </Lead>

  So we made a short overview video for every company in the S&amp;P 500.
  Each one runs about a minute, comes from the company&rsquo;s real filings,
  and plays like the stories kids already watch: Nike makes shoes and
  clothes, sells them all over the world, and that&rsquo;s where the money
  comes from. We generated them with AI and the data team so we could cover
  all 500 at once instead of hand-picking a few favorites.

  <OverviewPage />

  Then we went section by section. Every stat on the page got an AI insight
  next to it: what the term means and the so what. Under the growth chart, a
  line says how much Nike&rsquo;s revenue grew over the last four years and
  whether that&rsquo;s good. It&rsquo;s the learn mode idea from the old app,
  except it&rsquo;s always on and it&rsquo;s written for a kid.
</Section>

<Section no="04" label="Calling their own shots" title="Empowering kids to invest responsibly.">
  <Lead>
    Once a kid understands what a company does, the next question is
    whether or not they want to own a piece of it. We can&rsquo;t tell them
    buy or sell, but we can help them answer the question themselves.
  </Lead>

  We looked at how Warren Buffett evaluates potential investments and boiled
  it down to three simple questions:

    <Decide

  Each question comes with financial data and analyst perspectives to help
  answer it: how the company ranks, if it&rsquo;s been growing, and what the
  bulls and bears are saying. AI-driven insights help kids understand the
  &ldquo;so what&rdquo; of a data point or explain a term right where it
  comes up.

  At the end, it reads their own answers back. It might be a match and they
  should consider investing, or they don&rsquo;t think it&rsquo;s worth it
  right now, check out some other stocks and ETFs instead. We never pick for
  them. We called it &ldquo;Help me decide&rdquo;, and 1 in 10 kids use it on
  every detail page.

<Section no="05" label="Building the moat for kids" title="Their favorite companies in context in the market.">
  <Lead>
    Every trading app has a performance graph, P/E ratio, and ratings for a
    competitive audit. Nobody had a good visual of a company&rsquo;s economic
    moat, let alone one for kids. I took that as a challenge, and I think I
    hit the bullseye (pun intended).
  </Lead>

  Each company sits in the middle and the competition surrounds it in rings:
  closest rivals, competitors, farther out. One glance tells you who&rsquo;s
  crowding Nvidia and who&rsquo;s off in the distance. Tap a competitor and
  you get why they sit in that ring, plus the top three reasons, generated
  from the data in plain categories like special technology, famous brand,
  or cheaper to make stuff.

  <MoatPlate />

  The word moat is nowhere on the screen. A kid can see which companies are
  exposed and which ones are insulated just by checking the bullseye, and
  that&rsquo;s the whole idea.
</Section>

<Section no="06" label="Charting a course to better data viz" title="Eng told me it was too difficult to build, so I did it myself.">
  <Lead>
    The design team and I wanted an interactive performance graph. That&rsquo;s
    a high-impact interaction and it should feel premium: press and hold,
    scrub across time, and haptics at each interval. Engineering came back
    and said it was too hard to build, but every trading app has it, and that
    annoyed me enough to go build it myself.
  </Lead>

  <Plate cap="Performance graph" tag="haptic scrub" caption="Scrub it, and each interval ticks. The shipped SwiftUI version does this under your thumb, with real haptics.">
    <ScrubGraph />
  </Plate>

  I paired with an engineer who gave me access to the real repo and a
  playground inside it. Using Cursor and Xcode, I generated and refined the
  interaction in SwiftUI against our actual codebase, state by state, until
  it felt right. Most of that code shipped, including the first haptic
  feedback anywhere in the app. The first time the graph felt alive under my
  thumb, I was hooked.

  I designed the interaction, specced the logic and taste, got the packages
  to eng and they implemented it. Once I saw the level of control and polish
  I could affect in actual production code, I could feel my role changing.
  Looking back, that was the beginning of how I work now as a design
  engineer.
</Section>

<Section no="07" label="The call" title="What we didn't ship.">
  Our most ambitious idea, *Grows with You*, didn&rsquo;t make it. It would
  have read the whole picture of a young investor, their experience, their
  spending, their family&rsquo;s financial context, and leveled the entire
  interface up with them: language, data density, and complexity all
  changing as investors learned and grew.

  Based on timelines, we made the hard call to design for the newest
  investor instead; simple enough for everyone on day one. I still believe
  in that adaptive version, and I think it&rsquo;s more than possible with
  the tools at our disposal today.
