<Section no="03" label="The system" title="Glanceable, until it shouldn't be.">
  The core model came down to three postures for one screen. Ambient, it&rsquo;s
  a bulletin board you read from across the kitchen. Active, it&rsquo;s a
  dashboard filtered per family member. Focused, it takes over completely for
  one task — with what we started calling, affectionately and then
  officially, *big dumb buttons.*

  <Plate cap="Plate 03 · one screen, three postures" fig="FIG. C" caption="The interaction model, working. Switch modes — this is the spec the whole product hangs off (canvas: team-library 453-342132).">
    <SurfaceTriad />
  </Plate>

  The trust layer came straight from the research: privacy was the top-ranked
  *function* in the survey, so the access model is adults manage, kids view,
  a PIN for anything private — ambient content stays un-gated, and the
  authentication prompt appears only at the boundary. We explored voice,
  fingerprint, and proximity for auto-auth, and struck the camera through
  early: a device that watches the kitchen was never on the table.
</Section>

  The prototype also settled the biggest technical argument. Greenlight had
  only ever shipped native apps; the hub needed to move faster than app-store
  releases allow. So this became the company&rsquo;s first real web app — first
  feature: account monitoring for adults protecting an elderly parent&rsquo;s
  finances — and I spearheaded Glow, a web design system extending our native

  estimate at the time, *&ldquo;a thousand percent&rdquo;* faster. Leadership
  noticed the speed and set the bar accordingly: ship fast, match the
  category — and out-craft it. The Apple of family organizers. Something in
  your kitchen you wouldn&rsquo;t be embarrassed by if you had guests over.

<Section no="05" label="The constraints" title="The best patterns came out of the weakest hardware.">
  <Lead>
    First-gen hardware is humble: the phone in your pocket out-computes it.
    Our first onboarding dream — a Bluetooth handoff that signs the hub in
    automatically — was too much stretch for the timeline. The replacement
    turned the weakness into the design.
  </Lead>

  <Plate cap="Plate 05 · paired onboarding" fig="FIG. E" caption="Step through it. Hub serves a QR; the phone does the work; the wall reads back progress live.">
    <TandemSetup />
  </Plate>

  Typing happens on the good keyboard. Computation happens on the good
  computer. And the hub never goes dark — it narrates your progress so the
  two screens feel like one flow. That QR-handoff paradigm outlived
  onboarding and now carries every complex action in the product. So did the
  *takeover* pattern — full-screen focus on exactly one thing — and the
  avatar system born in these flows: ten illustrated characters by nine
  colors, animating onto the wall as each family member joins.

  Hardware got the same treatment: we couldn&rsquo;t design the device, so we
  designed at its margins. I killed the 27″ (a television pretending to be
  furniture) and called the 15″. Mic holes moved from a corner to dead
  center, because an assistant you talk to should look like it&rsquo;s
  listening. We fought for a RAM bump — a designer arguing bill-of-materials
  in service of animation frame rates — and won. And when the factory&rsquo;s
  stock Android keyboard threatened the whole feel, I built a themed keyboard
  APK from an open-source privacy keyboard and loaded it on the device.
  They&rsquo;d said it couldn&rsquo;t change; given a working example, they
  re-themed it themselves. Persuasion by artifact, aimed at firmware.

  The boot animation shipped to Shenzhen as a folder of numbered PNGs, which
  is funnier than any tooling story I could invent. The volume buttons are
  backwards. I know. V2.
</Section>

<Section no="06" label="Ten to one" title="One designer, ten engineers, no line unshipped.">
  <Lead>
    Family Hub&rsquo;s design team was, functionally, me — roughly ten
    engineers to one designer on a new platform, a new framework, and a new
    design system at once. The hardest problem wasn&rsquo;t any screen. It
    was staying ahead of a build moving faster than any spec could.
  </Lead>

  The deliberate call: let development get ahead of design. I&rsquo;d drop a
  happy-path flow, engineering would run, and I&rsquo;d backfill edge states
  and polish behind them — the spacing, the input states, the weirdly
  formatted time-zone picker. Things only designers notice, caught by the
  only designer. That was scary. It also worked, because feedback got
  precise: when screens needed fixing I answered with redrawn Figma, motion
  files, or live edits in DevTools on a staging mirror that turned straight
  into tickets. I kept a hub on my desk that I could debug, test, and mirror
  — the point where the hardware, the software, and my own design
  sensibilities finally merged into one job. That&rsquo;s when *design
  engineer* stopped feeling like a title I was trying on.

  It ran both directions. My favorite review of the project came from an
  engineer, unprompted, about one token:

  What speed bought is real: the team hadn&rsquo;t written a line of Family
  Hub code at the start of 2026, and by mid-July devices were live on walls

  firefighting — live audits feature by feature, tokens patched where the

  tickets — and putting out fires as a designer was, unexpectedly, some of
  the most satisfying work of the year.
