<Section no="03" label="The system" title="Three modes: glanceable, active or private.">
  **Ambient** is the screensaver with a job. It tells the
  house what&rsquo;s next and who&rsquo;s where without anyone asking.
  **Active** is when you walk up and use it. Full dashboard, modal views,
  and you can filter the whole thing down to one family member. A workspace, but not in the desktop sense. Still more of a detailed consumption + quick action experience with handoffs to the mobile app.

  Then there&rsquo;s the gate. Privacy was the top-ranked function in our
  research, so adults manage, kids view, and a PIN sits between looking at
  the thing and actually doing something on it. Approving chores, moving
  money, editing the calendar. All configurable by parents.

<Section no="06" label="The tension" title="One designer, ten engineers.">
  <Lead>
    Functionally, the Family Hub design team was me. One designer, roughly
    ten engineers, on a new platform, a new framework, and a new design
    system all at once. The hardest problem wasn&rsquo;t any single screen.
    It was staying ahead of the build.
  </Lead>

  The call we made: let engineering run on happy paths while I backfilled
  states and polish behind them. That was scary and new, but it worked because the
  feedback stayed precise. Redrawn Figma, motion files, and a loop I
  started calling the live audit.

  I kept a hub on my desk that I could debug, test on, and mirror.
  I&rsquo;d load the staging build into Chrome DevTools and edit the real
  product live: the way calendar events stacked in the timeline, the
  sizing of the assistant, pieces of the navigation. Every fix went into a
  ticket with the exact values already worked out. The audit caught
  quieter things too, values hardcoded where a design token should have
  been, and I tied those back into the system.

  <Claim>That&rsquo;s when <em>design engineer</em> stopped being a title I was trying&nbsp;on.</Claim>
